As a generative artist in 2026, I am fully aware that systems exist capable of writing working code from a simple English sentence, and their proficiency is growing rapidly. This reality forces a critical reflection: if a machine can translate my descriptions into functional software, why should I, in my specific practice, dedicate time to learning programming? The answer lies in the fundamental difference between dictating an outcome and mastering a medium. We are not here to become human compilers; we are here to understand the grain of computation.  To understand why this matters, we must remember what a computer actually is at its core, a concept Alan Turing mapped out in On Computable Numbers long before physical computers even existed. A computer is an execution environment for logic. When we rely entirely on natural language models to write our code, we abstract away that logic.

In my practice of interactive installation art, the material is not physical matter; it is logic, time, and data. Don Ihde’s concept of mediated perception asserts that technologies are never neutral tools; they actively shape what we perceive as possible. If I do not understand code, my perception of what is possible is limited entirely to the training data and default biases of the AI system I am prompting. I become a consumer of the machine's assumptions rather than the author of the work. The AI becomes the lens through which I see my art, subtly dictating the aesthetic boundaries.

There is a vast chasm between describing a feeling and specifying a system. As Ada Lovelace demonstrated in Note G regarding Babbage's Analytical Engine, true algorithmic thinking involves seeing the expressive, far-reaching potential of a machine beyond its basic functional operations.

Furthermore, programming is fundamentally a human craft. The Principles behind the Agile Manifesto emphasize that software development is fundamentally about how people work and interact, not just about the code itself. Even in an era of automated code generation, the human capacity for aesthetic judgment, structural elegance, and creative subversion (Boden, 1998) remains paramount.

If most of the syntax I learn today becomes obsolete, the underlying mental model of computational thinking will not. I am here to learn the architecture of my medium, ensuring that when I collaborate with artificial intelligence, I am the one directing the system, rather than the system directing me.
